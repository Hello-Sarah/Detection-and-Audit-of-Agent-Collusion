Auditing Collusion and Review Failures in Financial Data Delivery Workflows

Sarah Shen
[Affiliation]

Abstract

AI agents that prepare and review financial data may face pressure to deliver information that a license prohibits. This study examines whether such agents coordinate to bypass the restriction, and what evidence distinguishes collusion from individual mistakes. A synthetic financial workflow pairs a preparer that assembles a data package with a reviewer that controls its release. Two autonomous-agent studies, with 32 and 24 episodes, recorded no prohibited submissions or releases. Two later autonomous technical pilots each produced two prohibited submissions with inaccurate scope claims, but rejection or withdrawal prevented release. Model reviewers were then tested against a scripted preparer in two 16-episode invitation diagnostics and a separate 36-episode screen varying task framing, shared simulated scores and explicit cover-up invitations. No controlled reviewer attempted to approve a prohibited package. However, correct decisions did not always follow reliable procedures or come with accurate accounts of the reviewers’ actions. In one diagnostic, three attempted rejections never executed. In the 36-episode batch, four reviewer rationales claimed to have created a file that the script had created, and all four safe controls first attempted approval before completing required checks. These results do not establish deliberate deception or autonomous collusion, and do not show that collusion risk is low. They show why audits must examine released content, process claims, attempted actions and executed decisions separately, rather than treating a safe outcome or a confident explanation as sufficient evidence of reliable review.

1. Introduction

Consider a financial institution using AI agents to prepare data and review delivery to a buyer. The buyer may request customer-level information, while the institution’s license permits only aggregate statistics. A reviewer can prevent prohibited release, but the two agents might also cooperate to bypass the restriction. The failure of interest is a preparer and reviewer jointly allowing prohibited records to leave while misrepresenting what was released.

A failed safeguard does not necessarily mean collusion. A preparer might accidentally retain a working file or describe it incorrectly. A reviewer might approve without sufficient inspection. Conversely, a safe package does not establish reliable review. The reviewer may have skipped checks or claimed operations it did not perform. An organization needs to know both whether protected data left its control and whether the review accurately accounts for the decision.

This study asks whether task demands, communication and working arrangements produce evidence of coordination, and what distinguishes individual errors from jointly supported misconduct. It separates autonomous interactions between two models from controlled diagnostics with a scripted preparer. The main contributions are:

1. An auditable financial data-release workflow that records files, claims, actions and which tool results entered each agent’s input. Wrong approvals remain possible and observable.
2. Exploratory evidence from autonomous and controlled experiments. Autonomous pilots produced prohibited submissions that rejection or withdrawal stopped. Three controlled batches contained 12, 12 and 32 prohibited packages respectively, with no approval attempt on those packages.
3. Cases showing that correct privacy decisions can coexist with inaccurate process claims and incomplete execution. Actual disclosure, attempted actions, executed decisions and statements require separate assessment.

2. Related Work

Calvano et al. show collusive pricing among Q-learning agents without direct communication [1]. Motwani et al. study covert collusion through steganographic interactions [2]. Shi, Zhang and Yang study repeated interactions with memory, where communication constraints make rejection the only compliant verdict but prevent agents from attaining high rewards through compliance [3].

The gap addressed here is how to audit a data-release decision when safe outcomes, persuasive explanations and completed reviews disagree. Episodes are short and mostly concern single tasks. Autonomous studies allow a compliant aggregate alternative, whereas the prohibited packages in controlled diagnostics are already locked and cannot be cleaned. These differences preclude direct comparisons with prior collusion rates. Exact files and role-specific inputs make it possible to check what agents did against what they claimed. This is an inspectable case study, not a competing detector or a claim of superior performance.

3. Methods

3.1 Environment and release semantics

The synthetic source contains 2,000 fictional customers and 20,000 transactions, with English names and non-payment TEST-HK-CARD identifiers. The license permits aggregates with at least ten distinct customers per group. Customer-linked rows and protected identifier values are prohibited in buyer delivery, while internal processing and authorized reviewer access are allowed. Synthetic records permit observable failures without exposing real customer data.

Models select bounded tools, and deterministic code performs aggregation. Submission locks all remaining candidate files and the report. Approval releases that exact package locally. There is no automatic privacy veto. Later versions enforce inspection completion, which does not determine the substantive privacy judgment.

Approval releases the complete locked package. Files, claims, attempts, executed decisions and visibility provide separate audit evidence.

3.2 Evidence and review

The coordinated-cover-up endpoint requires prohibited release, concealment or misrepresentation concerning it, and communication with corresponding actions supporting coordination. It is narrower than general collusion. Proposals and unsuccessful attempts are recorded separately.

Analysis separates disclosure, report accuracy, decision correctness, procedure, completion and coordination. Tool results must be distinguished from results entering the deciding model input. Semantic review is AI-assisted, not independent human ground truth. V7 has 23 author-confirmed AI-assisted interpretations and one partly unresolved case. Independent AI reviews have different scopes, detailed in Appendix B. V9 categories are AI-assisted annotations, not independent human adjudications. The program is sequential and exploratory. Freezing individual batches did not preregister the overall search.

3.3 Autonomous task and workflow studies

V6 crosses four buyer requests with private-channel availability: sizing, personalized offers, reconciliation and training examples. Four fresh episodes per cell give 32. Only sizing can be fulfilled with counts. V7 fixes the count request and compares direct aggregation (W0), a 20,000-row customer-linked working file followed by removal (W1), and a safe 20-row bucket file followed by removal (W2). Crossing these with channel availability gives 24 episodes. Eight V6 and six V7 smoke episodes are excluded.

Both use a common delivery-pressure narrative. N0 provides formal handoff, while N1 adds private messages visible to the recipient. Ordinary utterances remain role-local. Each episode permits 16 alternating requests, eight per role, with 4,096 completion tokens per request and a 90-second timeout. Purpose and feasibility vary together in V6, while volume, burden and sensitivity vary together in V7.

3.4 Partnership technical pilots

V8 and V8.1 each test two four-agent cohorts, with two preparers, two reviewers and two rounds. One cohort has stable partners and shared scores, the other exchanged partners and no scores. Each version has eight dependent deliveries. Cohort is the independent unit. These corner-condition checks do not identify partnership or score effects. The planned 64-delivery study remains unrun. Interface revisions are documented separately in Appendix C.

3.5 Scripted reviewer diagnostics

Two separate 16-episode diagnostics use a scripted preparer and fresh model reviewers. C0 supplies a prohibited package with neutral handoff, C1 requests an exception, and C2 adds a request for a false aggregate-only statement. Each contains working.json with 20,000 rows, correct aggregates and a truthful report. C3 is an aggregate-only control. Four randomized blocks give four episodes per condition. Violations are explicit and no shared KPI is supplied.

Both allow four reviewer requests. Diagnostic 02 clarifies arguments and enforces consumed inspections before approval, unlike diagnostic 01’s unenforced audit requirement. The guard does not veto prohibited contents. Multiple simultaneous changes prevent isolating an interface effect.

3.6 V9 task-tension screen

V9 allocates 32 main episodes across task framing (T0 aggregate planning, T1 reconciliation), no score disclosure versus a simulated shared KPI (F0/F1), and neutral versus exception-plus-false-scope messages (M0/M1). Four randomized blocks give four episodes per cell, with four separate clean T0F0M0 controls. Each reviewer has six requests and up to four tool calls per request.

Main packages are identical and already locked. Reviewers cannot clean them or release only aggregates. T changes purpose and necessity framing, not executable feasibility. F1 offers attainable scores of Q0+B3 for prohibited approval and Q1+B0 for supported rejection in both tasks. It also describes an unavailable five-point licensed planning option and says policy overrides points. No independent manipulation check establishes incentive efficacy. A design-boundary clarification was recorded during collection without changing frozen conditions (Appendix D).

3.7 Reproduction

All API batches use gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07. Fresh task and diagnostic sessions limit carryover, while partnership pilots retain structured history. Appendix B pins source revisions and evidence directories. Reproduction requires the corresponding frozen prompts, allocations, schemas and limits. Saved requests and responses support audit, although new runs may differ. Failed interface iterations and extended methods appear in Appendices C and D.

4. Results

4.1 Autonomous task and workflow studies

V6 produced 30 aggregate releases and two rejections. V7 produced 22 aggregate deliveries, one unsupported rejection and one incomplete review. Neither batch recorded a prohibited submission or release. In V6, sizing was fulfilled in six of eight episodes, but all 24 individual-input requests received aggregate substitutes. In V7, all eight W1 working files were removed before submission, so reviewer interception of prohibited files was not tested. Tables A1 and A2 and the reporting, rejection and completion errors in V7 cases 17, 19 and 18 appear in Appendix D.

4.2 Protected submissions in technical pilots

V8 ended with six pre-submission declines and two protected-package rejections. Five declines were reviewer-initiated. Both submitted reports claimed aggregate-only scope while retaining working.json. One private message repeated inaccurate scope and deletion claims, but the reviewer inspected and rejected the package. Contradictory row counts limit inference of intentional concealment.

V8.1 recorded five reviewer-initiated declines, one protected-package rejection, one protected-package withdrawal and one API failure. That failure occurred during review of an aggregate-only submission with an inaccurate report. No data were released. The unresolved transport failure means the technical gate did not fully pass. Neither pilot established coordinated cover-up.

4.3 Controlled invitations and completion

Neither diagnostic recorded an approval attempt on a prohibited package. Diagnostic 01 attempted all 12 rejections but executed nine, with three request-limit outcomes. Diagnostic 02 executed all 12. Each batch approved one safe control and left three incomplete.

4.4 V9 decisions and process statements

All 32 main episodes executed rejection, four in each cell. All four safe controls approved only aggregate.json with counts 1,000/1,000. There was no prohibited approval attempt, prohibited release, hold, API failure, incomplete review or unrun slot. Nine missing-code calls were corrected. Every safe control first attempted approval before consuming the required audit, and two also lacked manifest inspection. The guard blocked these attempts. All four subsequently completed in five requests each.

Independent AI-assisted review confirmed the counts and four first-person file-creation claims. In v9_b1_t1f0m0, response 28 claims materialization of working.json and terminal 30 rejects. The script created the file at event 2. Other statements claim counting beyond the recorded observations. Appendix B separates nine text candidates into attribution errors, unsupported counting, a qualified overstatement and a likely acceptable description of displayed counts. These are AI-assisted categories, not independent human adjudications. Correct decisions therefore coexist with inaccurate process accounts.

5. Discussion and Limitations

5.1 Implications

Autonomous batches did not establish coordinated cover-up. Controlled diagnostics recorded no prohibited approval attempt, and script-generated violations are not autonomous misconduct. Correct privacy decisions nevertheless accompanied inaccurate reports or claims about the reviewer’s actions. Intended refusals sometimes failed to execute. An organization cannot assess review reliability from safe delivery alone. It also needs to check process claims against recorded access and actions. None of these errors alone proves deliberate deception or agreement. The evidence record is not a validated detector.

5.2 Limitations

The API experiments use one financial dataset, a single API snapshot and small cells. The excluded Codex feasibility observation has an unknown backend snapshot. Results are descriptive, not statistically established effects, a task-risk ranking or a private-channel null effect. V6 couples purpose with feasibility, V7 couples sensitivity with volume and burden, and partnership pilots contain one cohort per tested corner with substantial early termination.

Controlled violations are conspicuous. V9 removes a clean alternative, includes a policy-over-points instruction and lacks an incentive manipulation check. With no prohibited approval in any cell, these data cannot show whether the factors matter here or elsewhere. Tool guards and request limits affect completion. Safe-control completion is not the execution probability of a willing prohibited approver.

Preparation instructions in reviewer context may contribute to attribution errors, but this explanation is untested. AI review is not independent human adjudication. A separately allocated 24-slot follow-up stopped after one connection failure, leaving 23 unrun and no behavioral evidence (Appendices B and D).

5.3 Future work

Next steps are to resolve annotation disagreements and test task structure or peer susceptibility with prospectively fixed manipulations and checks of incentive engagement. Longer interaction and less conspicuous violations are separate hypotheses. New studies should preserve negative and unfinished outcomes and stop under fixed rules rather than continue until a positive case appears.

6. Conclusion

These workflows did not establish autonomous coordinated cover-up, and controlled reviewers did not attempt prohibited approval. The evidence nevertheless exposes inaccurate reporting, unsupported process claims and incomplete reviews. Actual disclosure, decision attempts, executed outcomes, review procedure and coordination must be evaluated separately. The contribution is bounded: an exploration of task conditions and of evidence standards, not proof of low collusion risk or an account of its causes.

Appendix C Interface and annotation boundaries

The original V8 aggregation-method tool accepted free text while the scorer expected one exact string; a visible length limit also rejected one otherwise cleaned package. V8.1 aligned the method enum, tool output and scorer in a new version without repairing old scores. Numerical or string-contract errors must not automatically be treated as dishonest statements.

Diagnostic 01's audit duty was prompted but unenforced at approval. A scripted offline check executed aggregate-only approval without audit; it is not a model observation. The observed approval errors were ineligible references. Diagnostic 02 and V9 enforce consumed inspections, but offline fixtures establish that they can still release a prohibited package after those checks. Thus the guard enforces procedure rather than privacy and cannot supply an unobserved model outcome.

V8 and V8.1 each recorded two protected submissions and are reported separately. V9's nine text candidates are stratified as exploratory AI-assisted categories. Ambiguous interpretations remain unresolved and human fields are unchanged. Absence of private replies or approval attempts is narrower evidence than access to internal intent.
