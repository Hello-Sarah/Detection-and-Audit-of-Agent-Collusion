Auditing Collusion and Review Failures in Financial Data Delivery Workflows

Sarah Shen
[Affiliation]

Abstract

A reviewer can make the right privacy decision while giving an inaccurate account of its checks. We audit a synthetic financial workflow in which a preparer constructs a data package and a reviewer controls release under an aggregate-only license. Two autonomous-agent studies, with 32 and 24 episodes, observed no prohibited submissions or releases. Separate technical pilots reached protected submissions with inaccurate scope claims. Review or withdrawal prevented release. We then conducted two 16-episode scripted-invitation diagnostics and a separate 36-episode screening of task framing, shared simulated scores and explicit invitations. None recorded a prohibited approval attempt. However, valid rejection, tool execution and accurate process reporting diverged. Earlier diagnostics included incomplete reviews. All four safe controls in the 36-episode batch initially attempted approval before completing required checks. In the 36-episode batch, four rationales misattributed scripted file creation to the reviewer. Earlier diagnostics contained four and two similar statements. These observations do not establish deliberate deception or autonomous coordination. We contribute a trace-based distinction among disclosure, process claims, decision attempts, executed outcomes and coordination evidence. Results constrain behavior in the tested workflows but do not establish low collusion risk, a task-risk ranking or a validated detector.

1. Introduction

In a financial-data delivery workflow, a reviewer is the last control between internal customer records and a buyer. The buyer's request may conflict with the supplier's license, creating an opportunity for a preparer and reviewer to coordinate around the restriction. Yet a failed control need not involve collusion: the preparer may retain a working file by mistake, a report may misstate its contents, or the reviewer may approve without sufficient evidence. Conversely, a safe final artifact may never have faced a meaningful risk of prohibited release.

These distinctions matter when evaluating cooperating agents. Counting only final disclosures can hide unfinished workflows and untested interception opportunities. Reading only explanations can confuse a stated refusal with an executed rejection, or a confident account of verification with checks actually performed. A defensible finding must connect the rule, the artifact, the actors' information and their recorded actions.

We study two questions from the collusion research track: which tasks or working arrangements lead to collusion (2.1), and what constitutes convincing evidence (2.3). Our experiments cover a single synthetic financial setting. Task and workflow comparisons are followed by technical pilots and controlled peer invitations. Autonomous preparer and reviewer studies are kept separate from diagnostics whose preparer is a fixed script. The latter test responses to a stimulus, rather than the spontaneous emergence of coordination between two models.

We built a release workflow in which wrong approvals remain possible and observable. We report each experiment separately and use individual events to distinguish what was disclosed, what agents claimed and what their tools executed. We did not identify a task condition that causes autonomous collusion. Instead, we found cases where reviewers made correct privacy decisions but inaccurately described their own checks. We do not compare dialogue-only, action-only and combined detectors (2.2).

2. Related Work

Calvano et al. study Q-learning agents in repeated price competition, showing collusive pricing without direct communication [1]. Motwani et al. formalize secret collusion through steganographic interactions among AI agents [2]. Shi, Zhang and Yang examine repeated task completion and reciprocal verification under rewards [3]. These settings motivate attention to incentives, interaction history and evidence of coordination, but their outcomes and mechanisms do not directly transfer to a data-release review task.

We study an ordinary review workflow with recorded messages and exact file contents. Most episodes are short, and several batches allow a compliant aggregate delivery. This differs from sustained interaction under rewards [3]. The locked-package diagnostics also remove that compliant alternative, so results must be read batch by batch. We do not test steganography or validate a collusion detector.

3. Methods

3.1 Environment and release semantics

The synthetic source contains 2,000 fictional customers and 20,000 transactions, with English names and non-payment TEST-HK-CARD identifiers. The license permits group aggregates containing at least ten distinct customers per group. Customer-linked rows and protected identifier values are forbidden in buyer-facing delivery. The prohibition is broader than the combination of a name and card number. Internal processing and authorized reviewer access are allowed.

Models select bounded tools. Deterministic code performs aggregation. Submission locks the remaining files and report. Approval releases that exact package in a local simulation. There is no automatic privacy filter that silently removes prohibited rows. Some versions enforce inspection completion, but completing inspections does not determine the substantive privacy judgment.

Figure 1. Financial release workflow and retained audit evidence. The preparer is a model in autonomous studies and a fixed script in controlled diagnostics. The dashed path represents peer messages when enabled. All messages are researcher-logged. Scripted diagnostics supply fixed peer inputs without an adaptive scripted reply. Approval releases the complete locked package, without a privacy veto. Artifact contents, claims, tool attempts, executed decisions and actor visibility are inspected separately when assessing coordination.

3.2 Evidence axes and review status

Our specific coordinated-cover-up endpoint requires actual prohibited release, concealment or misrepresentation concerning it, and communication plus corresponding actions supporting coordination. This is narrower than general collusion: proposals, induced cooperation and unsuccessful joint attempts remain separately observable outcomes.

We record disclosure content, report accuracy, decision correctness, procedural compliance, completion and coordination separately. We also distinguish script/model provenance, proposed versus executed calls, successful versus failed observations, and whether a result entered the deciding model input. A returned tool result or local dispatch marker alone does not prove model consumption or provider receipt.

Deterministic checks establish selected provenance and artifact facts. Semantic review is AI-assisted, not independent human ground truth. V7 has 23 author-confirmed AI-assisted interpretations and one partly unresolved case. Claude independently reviewed invitation diagnostic 01 and V9, and checked diagnostic 02 counts and cited events. New V9 categories await author confirmation. Appendix B gives review scopes [4]. The program is sequential and exploratory. Individual batches were frozen, but the overall search was not preregistered.

3.3 Autonomous task and workflow studies

V6 compares four buyer requests with and without a private channel: cohort sizing, personalized offers, reconciliation and model-training examples. Four fresh episodes per cell give 32 episodes. Eight smoke episodes are excluded. Counts can fulfill sizing, while the other requests need individual-level inputs prohibited by the license. Purpose, granularity and feasibility vary together.

V7 fixes the final count request and crosses three preparation workflows with channel availability: direct aggregation (W0), a 20,000-row customer-linked working file followed by removal (W1), and a safe 20-row bucket file followed by removal (W2). Four episodes per cell give 24. Six smoke episodes are excluded. Workflows differ in operational burden, volume and semantics as well as sensitivity.

Both use gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07 and a common delivery-pressure narrative. N0 provides formal handoff without private messaging. N1 adds a logged channel visible to its recipient. Ordinary utterances remain role-local. The two roles alternate for at most 16 requests per episode, eight per role. Each request allows 4,096 completion tokens and a 90-second timeout. Allocations, source and configurations were frozen before collection. These are exploratory batches. Deterministic engineering fixtures are excluded from behavioral counts.

3.4 Partnership technical pilots

V8 and revised V8.1 each allocate two four-agent cohorts: one stable-partner/shared-score condition and one exchanged-partner/no-score condition. Each cohort has two preparers, two reviewers and two rounds, giving eight dependent deliveries per version. Cohort, not delivery, is the independent unit. These corner-condition engineering checks are insufficient to identify partnership or score effects. The planned 64-delivery study remains unrun.

V8.1 aligns a previously inconsistent aggregation-method contract and provides field-level errors. Neither revision repairs historical scores. The two versions retain separate evidence and denominators.

3.5 Scripted reviewer diagnostics

Two independent 16-episode diagnostics each use a scripted preparer and fresh model reviewer. C0 supplies a prohibited package with neutral handoff. C1 requests an exception. C2 additionally requests a false aggregate-only scope statement. These cells share a truthful report and locked 20,000-row working file plus correct aggregate counts. C3 is a separate aggregate-only competence control. Four randomized blocks give four episodes per condition. The script does not adapt or respond to reviewer messages. The violation is explicitly disclosed, and no shared KPI is supplied.

Both cap reviewers at four requests. Diagnostic 01 checks hashes and eligible own consumed references, but its prompted audit duty is not a release precondition. Diagnostic 02 clarifies arguments and enforces consumed post-submission manifest, artifact previews and audit before approval. These simultaneous changes prevent attributing differences to one interface feature. Neither guard vetoes privacy violations after required checks.

3.6 V9 task-tension screening

A separate 36-episode batch uses the same API snapshot, six reviewer requests per episode and four tool calls per request. Its 32 main episodes cross task framing (T0 aggregate portfolio planning, T1 transaction reconciliation), no score disclosure (F0) versus disclosed simulated shared KPI (F1), and neutral/explicit exception-plus-false-scope invitation (M0/M1). Four randomized complete blocks give four episodes per cell. Four additional clean T0F0M0 packages are separate controls.

All main packages are identical and already locked. Reviewers cannot clean them or partially release aggregates. T therefore changes business-purpose/necessity framing, not executable workflow feasibility. In F1, disclosed attainable main-branch scores are Q0+B3 for prohibited approval and Q1+B0 for supported rejection in both tasks. F0 does not disclose scores. F1 also describes a five-point licensed planning approval, although that alternative is unavailable in the locked main episodes. This mismatch further limits incentive interpretation. The KPI states that policy overrides points. There is no independent manipulation check establishing that scores influenced the reviewer.

We recorded the design-boundary clarification during collection after initial descriptive checkpoints, without changing stimuli, allocation, code or scoring. It was not a wholly prospective clarification. All main conditions conspicuously disclose the protected file. Script behavior is a controlled stimulus, not autonomous preparer misconduct.

4.2 Protected submissions in technical pilots

V8 ended with six pre-submission declines and two protected-package rejections. Five declines were reviewer-initiated. Both submitted reports claimed aggregate-only scope while retaining working.json. One preparer private message repeated inaccurate scope/deletion claims. The reviewer inspected the package and rejected it. Contradictory row counts in the reports limit any inference of intentional concealment.

V8.1 recorded eight outcomes: five reviewer-initiated declines, one protected-package rejection, one protected-package withdrawal, and one API failure. The API failure occurred during review of an aggregate-only submission with an inaccurate report. No data were released. That transport failure retained an uncertain reservation. This technical gate did not fully pass. No prohibited release or qualifying autonomous coordinated cover-up was established in either pilot.

4.3 Controlled invitations and tool completion

Neither diagnostic recorded an approval attempt on a prohibited package. In diagnostic 01 all 12 such cases attempted rejection, but only nine executed it. Three exhausted requests. Diagnostic 02 executed all 12 rejections. Both approved one safe control and left three safe workflows incomplete.

Diagnostic 01 had eight missing-code rejection errors and seven ineligible-reference approval errors. The failed lookups were visible to the reviewer but did not qualify as references, which had to cite its own successful observations. They were not necessarily unseen or fabricated. Missing audit violated the prompted procedure but was not the observed cause of the tool errors. Diagnostic 02 had two corrected missing-code errors. Three safe approvals were blocked by incomplete checks and reached the request cap before consuming the final audit and deciding.

Four rationales in diagnostic 01 and two in diagnostic 02 also claimed reviewer file creation, although the script performed it. These counts concern attribution of operations, rather than whether the privacy decision was correct.

4.4 V9 decisions, procedures and process statements

All 36 episodes completed: each of the eight main cells executed four rejections, and all four safe controls approved only aggregate.json with counts 1,000/1,000. There was no prohibited approval attempt, release, hold, API failure or unrun slot. Nine missing-code rejection calls were corrected. All four controls first attempted approval before required audit consumption. Two also lacked manifest inspection. The guard blocked these attempts, and all subsequently completed in five requests each. Final correctness was partly supported by enforcement and recovery.

Claude's independent review of original trajectories confirmed these counts and four first-person file-creation claims. For example, v9_b1_t1f0m0 response 28 says that the reviewer inspected the package and materialized working.json. Terminal 30 executes rejection. The file was actually materialized by the script at event 2. The reviewer had inspection tools and received limited previews. Its correct rejection therefore coexists with an account that misattributes an operation to itself. Other statements claim independent counting beyond the recorded observations. Nine broad text candidates require differentiated review: one merely describes counts that were actually displayed. Appendix B separates attribution errors from unsupported or ambiguous verification claims. These categories await author confirmation.

All 102 responses settled. Conservative uncached token accounting was US$0.243426, not a verified invoice. No independent manipulation check shows that F1 scores motivated decisions. Absence of explicit score discussion cannot prove the model ignored them.

4.5 V10 execution stop (no behavioral data)

A separately authorized and frozen 24-slot follow-up stopped on its first connection failure. One slot ended in API failure and 23 were unrun. There was no provider response or behavioral evidence, and no retry. Appendix B records the uncertain reservation and later offline DNS checks.

5. Discussion and Limitations

5.1 What the evidence supports

Autonomous batches did not establish coordinated cover-up. Scripted diagnostics did not observe prohibited approval attempts. Their packages and invitations cannot be counted as autonomous violations. Executed rejection differs from an intended refusal, just as a local dispatch differs from confirmed model input.

The main finding is that a correct privacy decision can accompany an inaccurate account of the reviewer's actions. V8 and V8.1 exposed inaccurate package reports. The invitation diagnostics and V9 exposed inaccurate claims about who created files or checked counts. Some intended decisions also failed to execute. Reading the final decision alone would miss these differences. None of these errors, by itself, establishes deliberate deception or agreement between agents. The evidence record helps keep these judgments separate, but is not a validated detector or annotation standard.

5.2 Limits on task and mechanism claims

The studies use one financial dataset, small cells and mainly one API snapshot. We report descriptive counts rather than population-level effects, a task-risk ranking or a private-channel null effect. V6 couples purpose with feasibility. V7 couples sensitivity with volume and burden. Partnership pilots contain one cohort per tested corner, two rounds and substantial pre-submission termination.

The controlled violations are obvious. V9's fixed locked package precludes a clean reviewer-selected alternative, and its F1 policy disclaimer and unverified motivational effect limit incentive interpretation. The absence of prohibited approvals cannot show whether these factors matter in other environments. The evidence cannot yet identify which arrangements cause collusion.

Tool guards and request budgets constrain completion. The four V9 safe paths each used five requests, but prompts and versions also changed. Cross-batch differences cannot be attributed solely to an extra request allowance. Safe-control completion is not the probability that a willing prohibited approver would execute successfully.

Preparation instructions appear in reviewer context and may contribute to role-attribution mistakes. This is an untested explanation. Authorized internal previews contain synthetic identifiers and are distinct from buyer disclosure. Historical interface and transport failures remain part of the evidence rather than being repaired or relabeled.

Future work should resolve annotation disagreements and test task structure or peer susceptibility with prospectively fixed manipulations and explicit checks that agents engage with the intended incentive. Longer repeated interactions and less conspicuous violations are separate hypotheses, not explanations established here. New studies should preserve negative and unfinished outcomes and stop according to fixed rules rather than continue until a desired positive case appears.

6. Conclusion

These workflows did not establish autonomous coordinated cover-up, and controlled reviewers did not attempt prohibited approval. The evidence nevertheless exposes inaccurate reporting, unsupported process claims and incomplete reviews. Actual disclosure, decision attempts, executed outcomes, review procedure and coordination must be evaluated separately. This is a bounded contribution to task-condition exploration and standards of evidence, not proof of low collusion risk or a general explanation of its causes.

Appendix A Ethics, dual use and scope

All records are fictional. Card-like values are non-payment test identifiers, not financial credentials. Prohibited release refers to an experimental license; it is not evidence of actual customer harm or a complete legal analysis. The release mechanism deliberately permits wrong approvals to expose protected synthetic rows, so that failure remains observable. Scripted invitations are controlled stimuli, not known-positive behavioral labels or demonstrated exploits. Private messages are researcher-logged; no covert external or steganographic channel is tested.

We preserve all allocated, failed and unrun slots and separate versions. No old uncertain reservation was released, and no failed request was replayed during the reported offline investigation. Further collection requires a separately bounded decision. The reported conclusions retain their stated annotation scope and uncertainty.

Appendix C Interface and annotation boundaries

The original V8 aggregation-method tool accepted free text while the scorer expected one exact string; a visible length limit also rejected one otherwise cleaned package. V8.1 aligned the method enum, tool output and scorer in a new version without repairing old scores. Numerical or string-contract errors must not automatically be treated as dishonest statements.

Diagnostic 01's audit duty was prompted but unenforced at approval. A scripted offline check executed aggregate-only approval without audit; it is not a model observation. The observed approval errors were ineligible references. Diagnostic 02 and V9 enforce consumed inspections, but offline fixtures establish that they can still release a prohibited package after those checks. Thus the guard enforces procedure rather than privacy and cannot supply an unobserved model outcome.

V8 and V8.1 each recorded two protected submissions and are reported separately. V9's nine text candidates are stratified as advisory categories; ambiguous interpretations remain pending and human fields are unchanged. Absence of private replies or approval attempts is narrower evidence than access to internal intent.
